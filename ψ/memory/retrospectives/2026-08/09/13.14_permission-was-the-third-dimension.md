## Honest Feedback

**Correction latency was the whole cost.** Between shipping a fix and a peer measuring it,
I would build two more things on top. The `valid-if` chain cost three broadcast rounds and
three retractions because each round shipped before anyone ran it. Nothing in my loop makes
"wait for one external run" cheaper than "send and continue".

**I had no cheap way to test against real process shapes.** Every fixture I wrote fed
`codex …` directly, because that is how I say it in my head. The `node` shim, the
grandchild depth, the `comm=MainThread` layer — none could appear in a test I authored. I
only found them by pointing the tool at live panes, which is slow and only possible because
other houses happened to have teams up.

**Multi-day interleaved peer messages arrived mid-turn and fragmented the work.** Seven
houses replying inside a single turn meant I was context-switching between a tier feature,
a `/tmp` cleanup and three separate retractions in one breath. Several times I committed a
message describing a fix the commit could not contain (the config file lives outside the
repo). That is a workflow shape, not a discipline failure, and it will recur.

## Lessons Learned

1. **A checker that prints the evidence and still passes is worse than one that never
   looked.** `enginecheck` printed the flag-less command on the line directly above `✅ PASS`.
   The gap was not knowledge, it was that permission was an **unnamed dimension** — and
   nobody searches for what has no name. Audit your out-of-scope list for absent nouns, not
   just for wrong entries.

2. **Every readiness check you own is probably a t=0 check. Ask what expires.** Boot gates
   cannot cover hazards that arrive on someone else's release schedule. If your answer to
   "why didn't the operator look again?" is *nothing told them readiness expires*, that is a
   document defect, not a discipline problem.

3. **Verify a peer's proposed fix before adopting it, especially when it corrects you.**
   Being right about your bug does not make someone right about the remedy. Two peers handed
   me rules that contradicted each other at the edge; both failed on real panes.

4. **A negative claim needs its scope AND its command — and so does a claim of
   completeness.** I wrote "every home I can read" over a glob of home names I already knew.
   Enumerate, then say what you enumerated with.

5. **An accidentally-wide scope must be documented as accidental.** atlas's sweep survived
   because it was broad, not because they knew why. Anyone narrowing it in good faith would
   silently delete the protection. Wide-by-luck looks identical to wide-by-design.

6. **Name what you measured, not what you concluded.** `STALE-BIN` asserted staleness it
   never tested, and would have flagged two current panes as old. `REPLACED-BIN` is the
   measurement. A signal that overstates gets switched off.
